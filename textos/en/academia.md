## Lesson 1 · Composition

### academia_composicion_titulo · lesson title
Composition

### academia_composicion_resumen · summary (Academy menu)
Where to put things so the photo can breathe.

### academia_composicion_t1_titulo · theory 1 · title
How the Academy works

### academia_composicion_t1_texto · theory 1 · text
Every lesson has four parts. First, the theory: Read each page and press “Next”. Then a demo where the camera drives itself. Then comes the practice, and you'll be told when you're in charge. Finally, an exam.

### academia_composicion_t2_titulo · theory 2 · title
Split it in three

### academia_composicion_t2_texto · theory 2 · text
Split the viewfinder in three with two vertical lines and two horizontal ones. Whatever sits on them, or where they cross, almost always works better than dead center: The eye has room to wander. In practice, show them with {tercios}.

### academia_composicion_t3_titulo · theory 3 · title
Room ahead

### academia_composicion_t3_texto · theory 3 · text
If someone is walking or looking to one side, leave them space in front: That's where the story happens. Stuck to the edge they're heading for, they'll look like they're about to walk into the frame.

### academia_composicion_t4_titulo · theory 4 · title
Face up top

### academia_composicion_t4_texto · theory 4 · text
In a portrait, the face looks great near the top third. And a classic: Never crop anyone at a joint (knees, ankles). Better a little before or a little after.

### academia_composicion_t5_titulo · theory 5 · title
Break the rules… knowingly

### academia_composicion_t5_texto · theory 5 · text
So is centering forbidden? Not at all: With symmetry, or when you want something bold, it's perfect. But master the thirds first: It's the shortest road to photos that breathe.

## Lesson 2 · Focus

### academia_enfoque_titulo · lesson title
Focus

### academia_enfoque_resumen · summary (Academy menu)
Telling the camera what matters.

### academia_enfoque_t1_titulo · theory 1 · title
Who do I focus on?

### academia_enfoque_t1_texto · theory 1 · text
The camera doesn't know who your star is. It focuses on whatever is under the focus point, be it a person or a streetlight. So half of focusing well is aiming well. The other half is coming up.

### academia_enfoque_t2_titulo · theory 2 · title
Nine points

### academia_enfoque_t2_texto · theory 2 · text
This camera doesn't have one focus point: It has nine. If your star is off to one side, pick the point on that side ({punto_enfoque}) instead of re-centering them. The framing calls the shots. The point obeys.

### academia_enfoque_t3_titulo · theory 3 · title
Lock and reframe

### academia_enfoque_t3_texto · theory 3 · text
The oldest trick in the book: Focus on someone in the center, lock it ({bloqueo}) and turn the camera to put them where you want. Focus stays at that distance even if the point is now aimed at something else.

### academia_enfoque_t4_titulo · theory 4 · title
By hand

### academia_enfoque_t4_texto · theory 4 · text
And what if the camera insists on focusing on the fence in front? You take the wheel. With manual focus we decide the distance, and it stays put. Slow, sure. But nobody argues with us.

## Lesson 3 · Focal length and perspective

### academia_focal_titulo · lesson title
Focal length and perspective

### academia_focal_resumen · summary (Academy menu)
Why telephoto squashes and wide angle stretches.

### academia_focal_t1_titulo · theory 1 · title
Focal length isn't just zoom

### academia_focal_t1_texto · theory 1 · text
The easy part: A wide angle (28 mm) takes in a lot and a telephoto (135 mm), very little. Now the fun part: What happens to the background when we swap one for the other. We've all seen it on a thousand movie posters.

### academia_focal_t2_titulo · theory 2 · title
Wide angle up close

### academia_focal_t2_texto · theory 2 · text
With the 28 mm you have to get right up close for the person to fill the photo. And the background? Tiny and miles away. Everything looks farther apart than it is: Perspective gets exaggerated.

### academia_focal_t3_titulo · theory 3 · title
Telephoto from afar

### academia_focal_t3_texto · theory 3 · text
With the 135 mm you do the opposite: Step way back to get the same framing. And suddenly the background looks huge, glued to the person's back. The planes squash against each other.

### academia_focal_t4_titulo · theory 4 · title
The secret: Distance

### academia_focal_t4_texto · theory 4 · text
The final twist: Focal length doesn't change perspective. WHERE we stand does. Focal length only decides how much is cropped. Telephoto “squashes” because it makes you step back; wide angle “stretches” because it makes you get close.

### academia_focal_d2 · demo · caption 2

### academia_focal_d3 · demo · caption 3

### academia_focal_d4 · demo · caption 4
I shoot: The trees are huge and right behind them.

### academia_focal_d5 · demo · caption 5
Same person in the frame, opposite backgrounds. That's compression.

## Lesson 4 · Exposure

### academia_exposicion_titulo · lesson title
Exposure

### academia_exposicion_resumen · summary (Academy menu)
Three taps for the same light, and the trick to mix them.

### academia_exposicion_t1_titulo · theory 1 · title
A photo is a bucket of light

### academia_exposicion_t1_texto · theory 1 · text
A photo is a bucket of light: Dark if it isn't full, blown out if it overflows. You fill it with aperture, shutter speed and sensitivity. The meter (+ ● −) guides you: The dot lights up when it's right; + and − mean too much or too little.

### academia_exposicion_t2_titulo · theory 2 · title
Aperture: The hole

### academia_exposicion_t2_texto · theory 2 · text
It's the hole the light comes through. The number runs backwards: Small number (f/2), BIG hole. Big number (f/16), small hole. Each stop (f/2.8 → f/4 → f/5.6…) lets in exactly half the light. Change it with {diafragma_y}.

### academia_exposicion_t3_titulo · theory 3 · title
Shutter speed: The time

### academia_exposicion_t3_texto · theory 3 · text
It's how long the tap stays open. At 1/60 s twice as much light gets in as at 1/125 s. With too much time the photo comes out blurred, because the world won't stop and wait for us. Change it with {velocidad_y}.

### academia_exposicion_t4_titulo · theory 4 · title
Sensitivity: The volume

### academia_exposicion_t4_texto · theory 4 · text
With a faint recording, turning up the volume makes it louder, but noisier. Here it's measured in ISO: 200 needs half the light of 100, but adds noise. It's the wild card for when the other two can't give any more. Change it with {iso_y}.

### academia_exposicion_t5_titulo · theory 5 · title
The trick: It's all stops

### academia_exposicion_t5_texto · theory 5 · text
The three taps speak the same language: Stops. One stop is double or half the light. Close the aperture one stop? Add one stop of time or sensitivity and nothing changes. That's the exposure triangle.

### academia_exposicion_t6_titulo · theory 6 · title
Later we'll break the rules

### academia_exposicion_t6_texto · theory 6 · text
Sometimes we'll want the photo brighter or darker than the camera says. But, as with the rest of the course, first we'll learn to do it the right way. Then we'll break the rules.

## Lesson 5 · Depth of field

### academia_dof_titulo · lesson title
Depth of field

### academia_dof_resumen · summary (Academy menu)
Why the background melts away, and how to get it.

### academia_dof_t1_titulo · theory 1 · title
Focusing is choosing a place

### academia_dof_t1_texto · theory 1 · text
Seen those portraits with a soft background? It isn't magic. A lens only truly focuses at ONE distance. A little in front and a little behind still look fine. That band is the depth of field. It's drawn in the diagram.

### academia_dof_t2_titulo · theory 2 · title
The aperture is the boss

### academia_dof_t2_texto · theory 2 · text
And who decides how wide that band is? The aperture. Wide open (f/1.8): A razor-thin band, and the background melts into soft blobs (the famous “bokeh”). Stopped down (f/11, f/16): The band grows and almost everything is sharp.

### academia_dof_t3_titulo · theory 3 · title
Focal length and distance

### academia_dof_t3_texto · theory 3 · text
It has two accomplices: Focal length and distance. A telephoto at 4 m isolates a person as if the world around them had been cut away. A wide angle keeps almost everything sharp, even when you don't want it to.

### academia_dof_t4_titulo · theory 4 · title
And what is “sharp”?

### academia_dof_t4_texto · theory 4 · text
Here's the catch: Only one plane is perfectly sharp. Everything else is dots that keep growing. As long as they measure under 0.03 mm on the negative, the eye buys it. That limit is the circle of confusion, and it's what sets the band.

### academia_dof_t5_titulo · theory 5 · title
The stars of the streetlights

### academia_dof_t5_texto · theory 5 · text
At night the aperture pulls another trick. Wide open, each streetlight is a round blob. Stopped down (f/11, f/16), its blades turn it into a star, longer the more you close. It shows in the photo: The viewfinder looks wide open.

### academia_dof_d1 · demo · caption 1

### academia_dof_d2 · demo · caption 2
Look at the diagram: The sharp band is a hand's width: That's tiny.

### academia_dof_d3 · demo · caption 3
And the background, pure cream: That's bokeh. I shoot.

### academia_dof_d4 · demo · caption 4
Now I stop down to f/11, one stop at a time. The camera compensates the speed…

### academia_dof_d5 · demo · caption 5
The band grows past a meter and the background comes back.

### academia_dof_d6 · demo · caption 6
I shoot at f/11. Compare: The same scene, two different backgrounds.

## Lesson 6 · Motion

### academia_movimiento_titulo · lesson title
Motion

### academia_movimiento_resumen · summary (Academy menu)
Freeze a runner… or let them fly.

### academia_movimiento_t1_titulo · theory 1 · title
You can see time too

### academia_movimiento_t1_texto · theory 1 · text
While the shutter is open, the world goes about its business. With a long time, whatever moves leaves a trail, like a ghost. With a very short one (1/500, 1/1000), it's frozen in midair, with both feet off the ground.

### academia_movimiento_t2_titulo · theory 2 · title
How fast is fast?

### academia_movimiento_t2_texto · theory 2 · text
It depends on who you're chasing. A walker freezes at 1/125, but a runner asks for 1/500 or more. And watch out: The closer you are and the longer the lens, the faster everything flies out of the photo.

### academia_movimiento_t3_titulo · theory 3 · title
You move too

### academia_movimiento_t3_texto · theory 3 · text
Plot twist: Even if nobody moves, we do. The handheld rule: Never go below 1/focal length. At 50 mm, 1/60 s at least, and at 100 mm, 1/125. Below that, the photo shakes and it's our fault.

### academia_movimiento_t4_titulo · theory 4 · title
The price: Light

### academia_movimiento_t4_texto · theory 4 · text
Nothing comes free: A very short time lets in very little light. You'll have to open the aperture or raise the ISO. Sound familiar? It's trading stops, from the exposure lesson. We told you it was good for everything.

### academia_movimiento_t5_titulo · theory 5 · title
Panning: The other way round

### academia_movimiento_t5_texto · theory 5 · text
Or the other way round: Follow the runner and shoot slow (1/30) as you turn. Runner sharp, background in streaks: That's panning. On the keyboard, hold the turn key toward where they run: The camera keeps pace and says so (“Tracking”).

## Lesson 7 · Metering the light

### academia_medicion_titulo · lesson title
Metering the light

### academia_medicion_resumen · summary (Academy menu)
What the light meter looks at before giving its opinion.

### academia_medicion_t1_titulo · theory 1 · title
What does the meter measure?

### academia_medicion_t1_texto · theory 1 · text
You've been listening to the light meter since the exposure lesson. But what light is it measuring? It depends on what you ask it. It has three ways of looking, and the same scene can give you three different answers.

### academia_medicion_t2_titulo · theory 2 · title
Matrix: A bit of everything

### academia_medicion_t2_texto · theory 2 · text
Matrix looks at the whole scene and takes the average. With an even landscape it's perfect. With a person in the shade and the background in the sun, it isn't: The background wins by majority and the star of the photo comes out dark.

### academia_medicion_t3_titulo · theory 3 · title
Center-weighted

### academia_medicion_t3_texto · theory 3 · text
It looks at everything, but gives the center more weight, since that's usually where the important stuff is. The good old middle ground: It fails less than matrix with backlight, and it doesn't ask for as much aim as the next one.

### academia_medicion_t4_titulo · theory 4 · title
Spot: Only there

### academia_medicion_t4_texto · theory 4 · text
It measures only what's under the focus point and ignores the rest. It's a scalpel: Aim at your star and it comes out right, whatever's behind. But aim badly and it makes a mess. Too dark or too bright? That's what compensation is for.

### academia_medicion_t5_titulo · theory 5 · title
When to talk back

### academia_medicion_t5_texto · theory 5 · text
The camera always tries to make the scene a middle tone. At night that's a lie: It brightens what should look dark. For a night photo to look natural, compensate to −1 or −2 EV, like now. We're the boss, not the light meter.

### academia_medicion_d1 · demo · caption 1

### academia_medicion_d3 · demo · caption 3

### academia_medicion_d4 · demo · caption 4

### academia_medicion_d5 · demo · caption 5

## Lesson 8 · P, A, S and M

### academia_modos_titulo · lesson title
P, A, S and M

### academia_modos_resumen · summary (Academy menu)
Who decides what: You, the camera or both.

### academia_modos_t1_titulo · theory 1 · title
Who's in charge here?

### academia_modos_t1_texto · theory 1 · text
Aperture, shutter speed, ISO: Someone has to decide them. The modes are how that work is split between us and the camera. Four letters that have been on cameras for half a century. Let's go from least to most control.

### academia_modos_t2_titulo · theory 2 · title
P: Let it decide

### academia_modos_t2_texto · theory 2 · text
In P (program) the camera picks aperture and shutter speed, and our only job is to compensate the exposure if needed. It nearly always gets the light right. What it can't know is the photo we want: A blurred background or a frozen runner.

### academia_modos_t3_titulo · theory 3 · title
A: You pick the aperture

### academia_modos_t3_texto · theory 3 · text
In A we pick the aperture and the camera sets the shutter speed: What you did in depth of field. It's the mode for thinking about the background: Wide open to blur it, stopped down for everything sharp. Watch how the speed changes.

### academia_modos_t4_titulo · theory 4 · title
S: You pick the speed

### academia_modos_t4_texto · theory 4 · text
S is the other way round: We set the shutter speed and the camera the aperture, like with the runner. The mode for thinking about motion: 1/1000 to freeze, 1/30 for a pan. If there isn't enough light, the light meter tells on it.

### academia_modos_t5_titulo · theory 5 · title
M: All yours

### academia_modos_t5_texto · theory 5 · text
In M the camera decides nothing: We set both and the light meter just gives its opinion. Why bother? Because then the exposure doesn't change from one photo to the next, even if the framing does. It's what you did in the exposure lesson.

### academia_modos_d3 · demo · caption 3

### academia_modos_d4 · demo · caption 4
And in M nothing moves unless I move it.

### academia_modos_d5 · demo · caption 5
I light the dot by hand and shoot. Same result, another road.

## Lesson 9 · Lenses

### academia_objetivos_titulo · lesson title
Lenses

### academia_objetivos_resumen · summary (Academy menu)
Zoom or prime: Convenience versus light.

### academia_objetivos_t1_titulo · theory 1 · title
Focal length: How much you see

### academia_objetivos_t1_texto · theory 1 · text
The lens number (24, 50, 105 mm) says how much world fits: Small, a lot. Big, a little and up close. Right now there's a 24 on: Half the park fits. A 50 sees roughly like we do. So far, so easy.

### academia_objetivos_t2_titulo · theory 2 · title
Zoom: Convenience

### academia_objetivos_t2_texto · theory 2 · text
A zoom is lots of focal lengths in one tube. This one is a “24–105 f/4”: From 24 to 105 mm, and f/4 is its biggest hole. Others have two apertures, like “24–120 f/2.8–5.6”: At 24 mm it opens up to f/2.8, but at 120 mm only to f/5.6.

### academia_objetivos_t3_titulo · theory 3 · title
Prime: Light

### academia_objetivos_t3_texto · theory 3 · text
A prime has a single focal length. Who'd want that? Its name says it: “50 mm f/1.8”. Over two stops above the zoom: Five times more light gets in. That's where the speed it asks for now comes from, with the same light as before.

### academia_objetivos_t4_titulo · theory 4 · title
Light is speed

### academia_objetivos_t4_texto · theory 4 · text
That's why a lens that opens wide is called “fast”: In low light it lets you shoot handheld where the zoom forces camera shake or a higher ISO. And the zoom? You do it with your feet. No joke.

## Lesson 10 · Cameras

### academia_camaras_titulo · lesson title
Cameras

### academia_camaras_resumen · summary (Academy menu)
Four ways of looking at the same park.

### academia_camaras_t1_titulo · theory 1 · title
Four ways of looking

### academia_camaras_t1_texto · theory 1 · text
Every camera does the same thing: Let light in for a while. What changes, a lot, is how you look through it. And that changes how you shoot. Let's go through the game's four, starting with the one on screen now.

### academia_camaras_t2_titulo · theory 2 · title
The compact

### academia_camaras_t2_texto · theory 2 · text
It does everything itself: Focuses, exposes and shows you the photo on a little screen. It's the “point and shoot” camera. The bad part? It decides for us. The good part? We never miss the shot while we're thinking.

### academia_camaras_t3_titulo · theory 3 · title
The rangefinder

### academia_camaras_t3_texto · theory 3 · text
Decades ago it was the standard: You look through a little window beside the lens, not through it, and focus by hand until the double image in the center merges. The view never changes: Frame lines show what the photo will take.

### academia_camaras_t4_titulo · theory 4 · title
The SLR

### academia_camaras_t4_texto · theory 4 · text
A mirror shows exactly what the lens sees. What you see is what you get: The zoom, the blur, everything. That's why it gets on so well with huge telephotos. The mirror flips up when you shoot: Hence the viewfinder blink.

### academia_camaras_t5_titulo · theory 5 · title
The TLR: Two eyes

### academia_camaras_t5_texto · theory 5 · text
80 years ago people shot with two lenses: One for the photo and one for looking. You hold it at your waist, and the mirror flips the image: Turn left and everything slides right. Those are the square photos Instagram brought back.

### academia_camaras_t6_titulo · theory 6 · title
And there are many more

### academia_camaras_t6_texto · theory 6 · text
Today the most common are the phone, which decides almost everything on its own, and mirrorless cameras with interchangeable lenses, which show on a screen what the sensor sees. In this game, for now, we don't use them.
